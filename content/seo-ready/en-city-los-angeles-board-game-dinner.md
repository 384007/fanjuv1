# Los Angeles board gamers who are tired of the same three stores and the same three people Los Angeles has a massive board game scene, but it is extremely fragmented by neighborhood, by game weight, and by social vibe. The serious eurogamers, the party game crowd, the wargamers, and the “just here to drink and roll dice” people rarely mix. Once you are past the casual stage, you keep seeing the same faces at the same three stores or the same apartment nights, and it becomes hard to meet new people who are actually at a similar level and also want to talk about strategy instead of just the latest Kickstarter. A useful board game dinner here has to cut across some of those bubbles while staying narrow enough that people actually have things to talk about. ## The real Los Angeles constraints that matter for this hobby Traffic and distance are brutal. A dinner in Silver Lake for someone who lives in the Valley or Long Beach is already a real decision. Many serious players also have day jobs or families. A host who does not put a hard end time (and mean it) is not thinking about the actual humans they are inviting. The scene is also split between the “we just backed the new hotness” crowd and the people who have been playing for ten years and are tired of every event turning into a sales pitch for the latest campaign game. A dinner that does not filter for the latter will just become another place where everyone performs. ## Who actually shows up to a good one The people who respond to a real Los Angeles board game dinner are usually not the loudest or most social players. They are the ones who have strong opinions about specific designers, hate certain mechanics with a passion, and are tired of only ever seeing the same fifteen people at every open play night. They want to talk about real things — why certain games age well or fall apart after repeated plays, how the local scene has changed with the rise of certain stores, the economics of running events in this city — without it turning into either humble-bragging or recruitment for their own game night. ## Size and format that actually works Four to six people is the only number that consistently works when the topic is this specific. Eight and it splits into two conversations. The people who care enough about board games to drive across the city in traffic for a dinner want to actually talk, not make small talk with a big group. The best ones are at places where you can hear the person across the table and the service does not rush you. ## The detail that separates the real ones Before you commit, look for whether the host has named something specific they actually want to play or discuss. “I want to run a three-hour game of Root with the Riverfolk expansion and talk about why it clicks for some groups and dies for others” is a real signal. “Board game fans welcome, let’s play some games” is not. This format only works for people who are genuinely embedded in the Los Angeles board game world and want one contained, honest conversation with a few peers. It is not for casual players looking for a fun night out or people looking to recruit for their own weekly group. The value is narrow and specific. In a city where traffic, parking, and the constant pressure to be “on” already take a lot of energy, the dinners that survive are the ones that are honest about exactly how narrow that value is. | Fanju app
